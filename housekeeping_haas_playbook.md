# When housekeeping fails: a diagnostic guide in campus terms

*The failure taxonomy and the remedies below are Robert Haas's from his talk on understanding and fixing autovacuum (PGConf.EU 2023). They are used here as a skeleton to showcase the explanatory edge of the campus vocabulary laid out in the mapping document, with the actual PostgreSQL terms named alongside.*

> *Vacuuming is like exercise: if it hurts, you're not doing enough of it.* — Robert Haas

The word "vacuum" at face value suggests a cleaner at work, and clearing out stuff left behind by PostgreSQL's MVCC storage engine after deletes or updates is indeed one of the things a housekeeping round does — but there are further unrelated jobs performed when a housekeeper visits a room, and the failures that bring people to a guide like this are failures of any one of those, too often conflated or confused with the first. So before looking at the failure modes individually, it is worth walking the campus alongside a housekeeper to see them at work. Once the jobs are properly conceptually separated, failure modes aren't lumped under one cleaning image to let the correct fix for each of them come into view.

## What a housekeeping round does

A housekeeper — one of the autovacuum workers — is sent by the head of housekeeping (`autovacuum launcher`) into a building and walks it room by room. In each room there are up to three jobs to be done, and the head sends them on a fourth errand besides.

**Clearing.** When a tenant leaves the campus or moves to another room, what they leave behind does not simply vanish: their belongings sit in the old room until someone carries them out, which is the housekeeper's job. The task also involves marking which rooms now have space to spare on the register so that arriving tenants can be shown straight to them. This is the job the word "vacuum" names, and the one everyone pictures. When it falls behind, the building fills with the possessions of people long gone, which is known as bloat: because no one has recorded where the space is, new arrivals are sent off to fresh rooms instead of the empty ones, and the building grows larger than justified by the space occupied by its tenants and their aggregate belongings.

**Stamping.** Every tenant is issued a registration number on arrival, drawn from a pool that is vast but finite and arranged in a circle, so that when the numbers reach the end they wrap around to the start. The registration office works out which of two tenants arrived first from their places on that circle. That comparison holds only while no number is so old that it has fallen more than halfway around the ring — at which point it starts to read as though it came from the future, and the registration office can no longer tell old from new. To prevent this, the housekeeper stamps the long-resident tenants permanent: their temporary number is struck off and replaced with a mark meaning, in effect, "old; never mind the comparison." Each building keeps track of its oldest still-unstamped number, and the campus watches the single worst such number across all its buildings. Should that number drift too near the wrap, the registration office closes the building for new arrivals, moves, or departures, the building able only to answer questions, staying closed until the stamping has caught up.

**Updating the board.** Every floor has a board in its lobby recording, room by room, two facts: whether a room is *settled*, meaning everyone in it is accounted for and there is nothing to carry out, and whether it is *completed*, meaning every tenant in it has already been stamped permanent. Two different kinds of worker read that board, each for its own reason. The next housekeeper reads it to avoid wasted work — a room marked settled can be walked straight past, so a round need not trudge through every room of a building each time it visits. And the front desk reads it to answer questions without climbing the stairs: asked where a tenant is, the desk locates the room in its records, and if the board says that room is settled it can give the answer on the spot, since nothing in a settled room can have changed out from under it. Let the board fall out of date — because a round never got to updating it, or because a flurry of changes keeps wiping rooms back to unsettled — and both readers suffer at once. Rounds slow down, because nothing can be skipped and every room must be walked again; and the front desk slows down too, because every question now means sending someone upstairs to check in person.

**Taking the census.** The fourth errand is not part of the round proper. From time to time the head sends a housekeeper to take a building's census — not to clear or stamp anything, but to sample who is living there and how they are distributed across the floors: so many of this kind on the third floor, so many of that on the seventh. The front desk uses the census to plan its routes. Asked to find a particular sort of tenant, a desk with a fresh census walks straight to the floors where they cluster instead of searching end to end; a desk working from a stale census sends people on long walks to the wrong places. The census keeps its own schedule and answers to its own threshold, which is why it can be in good order when the clearing has fallen badly behind, or badly out of date when everything else is fine.

Those are the jobs. Keep them in view, because the phrases people reach for — "vacuum is slow," "vacuum isn't working" — flatten all four into one, and the flattening is where the trouble starts. "Slow" is a statement about the whole round. "Nothing is being cleared" is a statement about a single job within it. The right response is entirely different depending on which you actually have.

## How the failures sort out

Haas groups the ways this goes wrong into a short list — slow, stuck, spinning, skipping, starved — and it makes a good skeleton, because it is drawn from the failures that actually happen rather than from the way the machinery is built. Slow and stuck turn out to be one trouble seen from the user's side, a round that will not end, so there are four situations to tell apart. Two are things going wrong with a round already underway. Two are the head failing to send a round at all. Hold that second pair together from the start, because from the corridor they look identical while their causes are not the same.

## A round that drags on (slow, or stuck)

A housekeeper went into a building and has not come out. Sometimes nothing has technically gone wrong — a large enough building takes that long to walk, and no adjustment changes arithmetic. But when something *is* wrong, remember what a round contains, because a slow round is not one problem but three. While a housekeeping round drags, the clearing falls behind and the building bloats; the stamping falls behind and the oldest unstamped number creeps toward the wrap; and the board stays out of date, so lookups stay slow. None of the three announces itself as "slow." They surface later — as bloat, as a wraparound scare, as queries that have lost their shortcut — which is why a slow round is worth taking seriously rather than waiting out.

The usual reason a round drags is the dullest one: the housekeeper has been told to work slowly, and is obeying. They work in a rhythm — a burst of work, a pause, another burst — and there are two ways to make them faster: a larger burst, or a shorter pause.

A pause can only be shortened so far, and the default already low — you hit a floor and stop gaining. The burst can be extended up to a high limit. Tell the housekeeper to do twice the work before pausing, or seven times, and the round speeds up by just that, predictably. So the better lever is usually the size of the burst (`autovacuum_vacuum_cost_limit`), not the length of the pause (`autovacuum_vacuum_cost_delay`).

> If a config was carried forward from before version 12, you'd want to consider an exception to the guidance to adjust the burst and leave the pause as it is. By the time version 12 was released, the storage that maintainers took to be typical had become fast enough that cutting the default pause tenfold made sense as a one-time correction. So an instance carrying a configuration specifying a pre-version-12 sleep duration is running on a pause meant for another era's hardware, and might want something closer to the shorter modern default.

If the housekeeper is not working slowly but not working at all — standing in a doorway, going nowhere — then they are waiting for someone else to move. Some other process holds something the round needs, and the round will wait however long that takes. The instinct here is to dismiss the idle housekeeper so the other party can get on, but that gains nothing: the head will send the housekeeper straight back to the same building, often within seconds, because by its reckoning the work still needs doing. Clear the other party instead. Two more causes are worth knowing on sight. A loading dock may be on its way out — a disk that was quick yesterday, is slow today, and will be at a standstill tomorrow. And a filing cabinet may be corrupted into a trap: a cabinet is read in order, each card naming the next, and if a card's pointer has been bent into a ring, the housekeeper follows it round and round and never reaches the end. A building cannot do this, since its rooms are walked plainly from first to last; so a round caught circling is caught in a cabinet, not among the rooms, and the cure is to drop the cabinet or build it fresh.

## Rounds that clear nothing (spinning)

This looks quite different from the corridor: not one round that never finishes, but the same building visited again and again, and none of the visits leaving it any emptier. As with a slow round, it can be ordinary — a building that turns over quickly genuinely needs frequent attention, and frequent visits are the system working, not failing.

When it is not ordinary, the most common cause is worth following all the way through, because it shows two of the jobs failing together for one reason. The housekeeper arrives, walks the rooms, finds the belongings of departed tenants exactly where they were — and cannot carry them out, because someone in the system might still need to see them. A transaction has been running long enough that, for all the system can tell, it may yet walk into any room and expect those tenants to be there; until it finishes, their belongings are held in place. So the clearing comes to nothing, visit after visit, and the building stays full. That is the obvious failure. The quieter one rides along with it: those belongings that cannot be carried out still carry their original registration numbers, and while they sit there the building's oldest unstamped number cannot advance past them. The stamping is therefore stuck as well — not because the housekeeper refuses to stamp, but because the building's count is pinned by the very things that cannot be removed. A building in this state is at once growing without limit and making no progress toward safety, and it will go on doing both no matter how often a housekeeper is sent, until the thing holding the tenants in place is dealt with.

Three things hold tenants in place this way. The first is a long-running transaction, the case just described; you find it by looking for who has been at work in the building far too long (`pg_stat_activity`). The second is a prepared transaction left unresolved — a piece of business set aside half-finished, for which the system must keep every option open, and which can hold tenants even in buildings it never entered (`pg_prepared_xacts`). The third is the one Haas singles out as astonishingly common: a replication slot orphaned by a failover — a standing order to preserve everything some standby might still want, left behind when that standby was retired and never cancelled (`pg_replication_slots`). None of the three is fixed by adjusting a threshold. Remove the blocker, and only then do the rounds begin to empty the building again.

## A building the head never sends anyone to (skipped)

The last two failures share one appearance — a building that plainly needs attention sits untouched — and because their causes are opposite, the first task is always to tell them apart. In the first, nothing is keeping the housekeepers away; the head simply does not believe the building needs anyone, and sends no one.

The head decides what needs doing from a ledger it keeps of how much each building has shed since its last round. On a large building the trouble is usually that this threshold is written as a proportion of the building's size rather than a fixed amount, and a proportion that is sensible for a small building is far too slack for a large one. A fifth of a small building is a handful of rooms; waiting until that much has piled up before sending a round is perfectly fine. A fifth of an enormous building is an enormous heap of accumulated junk, and waiting for it means the building bloats badly before the head ever judges a round to be due. The fix is to set a tighter proportion for that building alone (`autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor`, which defaults to a fifth and can be lowered per building), so the head reacts sooner. Two other possibilities are worth ruling out first, since they are quick to check. Someone may have switched housekeeping off for that building outright (`autovacuum_enabled = false`) and never switched it back on. Or the head's ledger itself may have been wiped — the running counts thrown away, most often by a deliberate reset of the statistics — after which every building reads as having shed nothing, and the head, seeing no work anywhere, sends no one anywhere. Sending a housekeeper in by hand restores enough of the counts for the head to begin judging again.

## A building no housekeeper can reach (starved)

In the second case the head would gladly send a housekeeper, but every one of them is already out on a round. The staff is small — three by default (`autovacuum_max_workers`) — and if all three are occupied for hours at a stretch, a building waiting its turn sits untouched, not because the head has overlooked it but because there is no one free to go. You tell this apart from a skipped building by counting heads: three housekeepers all long out means the campus is simply over capacity, and the building is starved rather than passed over. The remedy is the same lever as for a slow round — give the housekeepers a larger burst, so each round finishes sooner and releases its housekeeper for the next building. This is also the one failure of the four in which taking on more staff genuinely helps, since more housekeepers can be out at once; but more staff must come with a larger budget, for the reason that follows.

## Why hiring more staff usually backfires

The burst is not set for each housekeeper separately. It is a single allowance for the whole staff, divided among however many are out at any moment — and this catches almost everyone, because it turns the obvious move into the wrong one. Faced with rounds that take too long, the natural response is to put more housekeepers on. But if the allowance is left where it was, that same allowance is now split more ways, so each housekeeper receives a smaller share and works more slowly than before. You finish with more housekeepers, each one crawling, and the buildings no better served than when there were fewer of them. More staff answers starvation and nothing else, and only ever alongside a matching rise in the allowance: put on twice the staff, and raise the allowance at least twofold to go with them. One wrinkle is worth knowing. From version 16 onward, raising the allowance reaches housekeepers already out on their rounds; before that it took hold only the next time someone was dispatched, which left people watching a round stay slow no matter what they changed.

## Telling which failure you have

The reliable way through all of this is to stop guessing from how the building looks and watch what the housekeeper is doing. Most of what you need is in `pg_stat_activity`, in the columns that record what each worker is waiting on — read a few times in a row, because they describe only the instant you happen to catch. A housekeeper repeatedly caught in the pauses between bursts has been told to work too slowly; that is the throttle, and the lever is the burst. A housekeeper repeatedly waiting on the loading dock is held up by the disk, which cannot move things as fast as the round is asking of it, and no setting makes a disk faster than it is. The progress view (`pg_stat_progress_vacuum`) tells you which part of the round is underway, which is often enough to throw out a theory on the spot — a suspected cabinet-loop does not survive the discovery that the housekeeper is still down among the rooms, nowhere near the cabinets. And the bluntest check of all, whether the process is burning processor or disk or consuming nothing, separates a housekeeper who is working and merely slow from one who is standing in a doorway, waiting on something outside.

## On knowing how much junk is there

There is no way to learn exactly how much junk a building holds that is also cheap, and there probably never will be, because the two pull against each other. An exact count means walking the whole building room by room and tallying what you find (`pgstattuple`): accurate, and about as much work as a round itself. The cheap estimates avoid the walk by working from the census instead, which is what makes them quick and also what makes them unreliable — the census does not record everything, misses whole categories such as the contents of the warehouse, and can be out of date, so its figures are sometimes simply wrong. The sensible posture is to treat any bloat number as approximate, and, at the same time, to remember that a rough and occasionally wrong measurement is far better than the usual practice, which is to take no measurement at all and discover the bloat only when it begins to hurt.
